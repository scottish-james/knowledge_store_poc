## 1. Regulatory Framework

The UK mortgage market operates within a regulatory framework overseen by the [Financial Conduct Authority (FCA)](https://www.fca.org.uk/) . All mortgage advisors must hold appropriate qualifications and be authorised by the FCA to provide regulated advice.

The Mortgages and Home Finance: Conduct of Business sourcebook (MCOB) sets out the rules that govern how firms must conduct their mortgage business. This includes requirements around affordability assessments, disclosure, and the fair treatment of customers throughout the mortgage lifecycle.

### 1.1 Advisor Qualifications

To provide mortgage advice in the UK, individuals must hold a Level 3 Certificate in Mortgage Advice and Practice (CeMAP) or an equivalent qualification recognised by the FCA. The qualification framework covers three core modules:

1. UK Financial Regulation — covering the regulatory structure, compliance requirements, and the role of the FCA and Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA).
2. Mortgages — covering mortgage types, lending criteria, conveyancing processes, and the Scottish property purchase system including missives and LBTT.
3. Assessment of Mortgage Advice Knowledge — covering case studies, affordability calculations, and the application of regulatory principles to real client scenarios.

Continuing Professional Development (CPD) is mandatory. The FCA expects advisors to complete a minimum of 35 hours of CPD per year, of which at least 21 hours must be structured learning. Further details are available in the [FCA Training and Competence sourcebook](https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/TC/) .

### 1.2 Consumer Duty

Since July 2023, the FCA’s Consumer Duty has placed higher expectations on firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers. The Duty requires firms to act in good faith, avoid foreseeable harm, and enable customers to pursue their financial objectives. For mortgage advisors, this means:

- Ensuring product recommendations genuinely meet the customer’s needs and circumstances
- Providing clear, timely information that customers can understand and act upon
- Monitoring outcomes and taking corrective action where customers are not getting fair value
- Paying particular attention to vulnerable customers and those with characteristics that may affect their ability to engage with the advice process

## 2. Mortgage Products

The UK mortgage market offers a wide range of products designed to suit different borrower profiles. According to [UK Finance](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/) , gross mortgage lending in the UK reached £226 billion in 2024. Understanding the features, risks, and suitability of each product type is essential for providing sound advice.

### 2.1 Product Types Overview

| **Product Type**                 | **Key Features**                                                                                       | **Typical Term**                                       | **Risk Profile**                                                                                    |
|----------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Fixed Rate**                   | Interest rate locked for a set period. Monthly payments stay the same regardless of base rate changes. | 2, 3, 5, 7 or 10 years                                 | Low — payment certainty. Risk of early repayment charges if switching before the fixed period ends. |
| **Tracker**                      | Tracks the Bank of England base rate plus a set margin. Payments move with rate changes.               | 2 or 5 years, or lifetime                              | Medium — exposed to rate rises but benefits from cuts. Some have a collar (minimum rate).           |
| **Standard Variable Rate (SVR)** | The lender’s own rate, which can change at any time at the lender’s discretion.                        | No fixed term — reverts to SVR after initial deal ends | High — typically the most expensive option. No early repayment charges, so easy to switch.          |
| **Discount**                     | A percentage discount off the lender’s SVR for a set period.                                           | 2 or 3 years                                           | Medium-High — cheaper than SVR but still variable. Discount applies to SVR which can itself change. |
| **Offset**                       | Savings balance is offset against the mortgage balance, reducing the interest charged.                 | 2 or 5 years fixed, or variable                        | Low-Medium — good for borrowers with significant savings. May carry a slightly higher rate.         |

### 2.2 Loan-to-Value Ratios and Pricing

The loan-to-value (LTV) ratio is one of the most significant factors in mortgage pricing. The [Bank of England](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/) monitors LTV distribution across the market as a measure of financial stability.

| **LTV Band**   | **Deposit**   | **Typical 2yr Fixed**   | **Typical 5yr Fixed**   | **Availability**                           |
|----------------|---------------|-------------------------|-------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Up to 60%**  | 40%+          | 3.89%                   | 3.99%                   | Wide — all major lenders                   |
| **60–75%**     | 25–40%        | 4.09%                   | 4.19%                   | Wide — all major lenders                   |
| **75–85%**     | 15–25%        | 4.39%                   | 4.49%                   | Good — most lenders                        |
| **85–90%**     | 10–15%        | 4.79%                   | 4.89%                   | Moderate — selected lenders                |
| **90–95%**     | 5–10%         | 5.29%                   | 5.49%                   | Limited — fewer lenders, stricter criteria |

## 3. Affordability Assessment

Responsible lending is a cornerstone of the UK mortgage market. Advisors must carry out a thorough assessment of the borrower’s ability to repay, both at the initial rate and at a stressed rate. The FCA’s responsible lending rules require lenders to assess affordability based on the borrower’s verified income and expenditure.

### 3.1 Income Verification

Income verification requirements vary depending on the borrower’s employment status. The table below summarises the standard documentation required:

| **Employment Type**                      | **Documentation Required**                                                                                                          | **Additional Notes**                                                                                                                              |
|------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Employed (PAYE)**                      | Latest 3 months’ payslips, P60, and employer’s reference confirming role, salary, and contract type.                                | Overtime, bonuses, and commission may be included if regular and sustainable. Most lenders require a 6–12 month track record.                     |
| **Self-employed (sole trader)**          | 2–3 years’ SA302 tax calculations and corresponding tax year overviews from HMRC. Full accounts prepared by a qualified accountant. | Net profit is typically used. Some lenders accept 1 year’s figures for established businesses. Declining profits may trigger additional scrutiny. |
| **Self-employed (Ltd company director)** | 2–3 years’ company accounts, SA302s, and CT600 corporation tax returns. Dividend vouchers and director’s loan account statements.   | Some lenders use salary + dividends; others consider retained profits or net profit before tax. The choice of lender matters significantly here.  |
| **Contractor**                           | Current contract showing day rate, contract length, and confirmation of renewal history. 12 months’ contracting history.            | Specialist contractor mortgage lenders will annualise the day rate. Minimum 6–12 months’ continuous contracting usually required.                 |

### 3.2 Stress Testing

Lenders must assess whether the borrower can afford the mortgage at a stressed interest rate. The standard approach is:

- For fixed-rate products with terms of 5 years or more, affordability may be assessed at the pay rate (no stress test required by some lenders).
- For fixed-rate products under 5 years, lenders typically stress-test at the reversion rate (SVR) plus a margin of 1–2 percentage points.
- Variable and tracker products are stress-tested at the current pay rate plus 3 percentage points, or the lender’s stress rate, whichever is higher.

For detailed affordability guidance, refer to Section 1: Regulatory Framework and the FCA’s responsible lending rules in MCOB 11.

## 4. Scottish Property Purchase Considerations

The Scottish property purchase system differs from that in England and Wales in several important ways. Advisors working with Scottish borrowers must understand these differences to provide accurate guidance.

### 4.1 Land and Buildings Transaction Tax (LBTT)

Scotland has its own property transaction tax, LBTT, which replaced Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT) in April 2015. LBTT is administered by [Revenue Scotland](https://www.revenue.scot/) . The current residential rates are:

| **Purchase Price Band**   | **LBTT Rate**   | **Tax on Band**   |
|---------------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Up to £145,000            | 0%              | £0                |
| £145,001 – £250,000       | 2%              | Up to £2,100      |
| £250,001 – £325,000       | 5%              | Up to £3,750      |
| £325,001 – £750,000       | 10%             | Up to £42,500     |
| Over £750,000             | 12%             | Varies            |

An Additional Dwelling Supplement (ADS) of 6% applies to purchases of additional residential properties (such as buy-to-let or second homes) where the purchase price is £40,000 or more. The ADS is charged on the total purchase price, not just the amount above any threshold. For further information, see the [Revenue Scotland LBTT calculator](https://www.revenue.scot/land-buildings-transaction-tax/lbtt-calculator) .

### 4.2 The Offer and Missives Process

In Scotland, the purchase process follows a distinct legal path:

1. Home Report — The seller must commission a Home Report before marketing the property. This includes a single survey, an energy report, and a property questionnaire.
2. Noting Interest — Prospective buyers formally note their interest through their solicitor, ensuring they are notified of a closing date if one is set.
3. Formal Offer — The buyer’s solicitor submits a written offer specifying the price, proposed entry date, and conditions (known as ‘suspensive conditions’).
4. Qualified Acceptance — The seller’s solicitor responds, typically accepting the offer subject to amendments. This begins a process of negotiation.
5. Conclusion of Missives — Once all terms are agreed, the missives are concluded, forming a legally binding contract. Unlike in England, neither party can withdraw without significant financial consequences.
6. Settlement — On the agreed date of entry, the buyer’s solicitor transfers the funds, and the seller’s solicitor releases the keys. Title is registered with Registers of Scotland.

#### 4.2.1 Bridging Finance Considerations

Where a buyer is selling one property and purchasing another, the binding nature of Scottish missives creates a risk if the two transactions do not settle on the same date. Bridging finance may be required to cover the gap. Advisors should ensure clients understand the costs and risks involved, including arrangement fees, interest charges, and the possibility of extended bridging periods if the sale chain breaks.

## 5. Buy-to-Let Mortgages

Buy-to-let (BTL) mortgages are designed for borrowers purchasing property to rent out. They differ from residential mortgages in several key ways:

| **Feature**             | **Residential Mortgage**                                                    | **Buy-to-Let Mortgage**                                                                                      |
|-------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Purpose**             | Owner-occupied primary residence                                            | Property let to tenants for rental income                                                                    |
| **Affordability basis** | Borrower’s income and expenditure                                           | Rental income (typically 125–145% of mortgage payment at a stressed rate)                                    |
| **Minimum deposit**     | 5–10% (depending on scheme)                                                 | 25% (some lenders require 20% for limited ranges)                                                            |
| **Interest rates**      | Lower — typically 0.5–1.0% below BTL rates                                  | Higher — reflecting the increased risk to the lender                                                         |
| **Regulation**          | FCA regulated                                                               | Mostly unregulated (except consumer BTL, where the borrower or a family member may occupy the property)      |
| **Tax treatment**       | No income tax on own home; Capital Gains Tax exemption on primary residence | Rental income taxable; mortgage interest relief restricted to basic rate tax credit; CGT payable on disposal |

For Scottish BTL purchases, the Additional Dwelling Supplement adds a significant cost. See Section 4.1: LBTT for current rates.

## 6. Government Schemes and Support

Several government-backed schemes aim to help first-time buyers and those on lower incomes get onto the property ladder.

### 6.1 First-Time Buyer Relief (Scotland)

First-time buyers in Scotland benefit from LBTT relief that raises the nil-rate band from £145,000 to £175,000, providing a maximum saving of £600. To qualify, the buyer must not have previously owned a residential property anywhere in the world.

### 6.2 Shared Equity Schemes

The Scottish Government operates shared equity schemes through [Homes for Scotland](https://www.mygov.scot/help-buying-home) . These schemes provide an equity loan of up to 15% of the property value, reducing the mortgage and deposit required. The equity loan is repayable on sale or after 25 years.

### 6.3 Lifetime ISA

The Lifetime ISA allows individuals aged 18–39 to save up to £4,000 per year towards a first home (or retirement). The government adds a 25% bonus on contributions, up to £1,000 per year. The property must cost £450,000 or less. Full details are available from [HMRC](https://www.gov.uk/lifetime-isa) .
